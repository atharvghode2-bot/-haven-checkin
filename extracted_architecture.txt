AI Mental Health Screening Chatbot
System Architecture
Mini-project  |  B.Tech CSE (Data Science)
1. Overview
The app is a conversational check-in assistant for students. It asks questions adapted from validated screening instruments (PHQ-9 and GAD-7 style), accepts typed or spoken answers, and optionally reads facial expression through the camera. An NLP/LLM engine scores each session for stress, anxiety and risk. Low-risk sessions end with self-help tips; high-risk sessions send a confidential alert to a counselor, who sees an anonymous ID and mood trends on a dashboard.
The system has three input signals (questionnaire answers, text/voice sentiment, and facial emotion) that are combined into one risk score. It is a screening aid, not a diagnostic tool.

4. Check-in data flow
The student opens the app, accepts the consent screen, and turns the camera on if they want to.
The student answers each question by typing or speaking. The browser sends the answers to the backend.
In parallel, the face model runs locally in the browser and sends averaged emotion scores for each question.
The backend passes the text (or transcript) to the AI engine for sentiment and risk scoring.
The fusion step combines all scores, saves the results in MongoDB and decides whether to raise an alert.
Low-risk sessions close with self-help tips. High-risk sessions alert the counselor and show a helpline to the student.
5. Face emotion module (on-device)
Facial emotion recognition runs entirely inside the student's browser. The video never leaves the device, and only emotion scores such as {sad: 0.62, neutral: 0.30} are sent to the backend.
How it works
Camera: navigator.mediaDevices.getUserMedia() after explicit consent; one frame is sampled every 1-2 seconds while the student answers.
Model: face detection followed by an expression classifier (happy, sad, angry, fearful, neutral, surprised) using face-api.js, TensorFlow.js or MediaPipe Face Landmarker.
Output: scores averaged over each question and sent with the answer. No images or video are uploaded or stored.
Evaluation: FER2013, AffectNet or CK+ can be used to measure accuracy for the project report.
Why on-device
Privacy: facial images of students are never transmitted or stored.
Lower server load and no video upload bandwidth.
Easier to justify in a mental health setting, where trust matters.
Limitations
Facial emotion models are only moderately accurate (about 65-75% on FER2013) and do worse with real webcam footage.
A face shows expression, not feeling. Someone can look neutral while anxious, so this is a weak signal.
Models can be biased across skin tones, lighting and glasses. The app must work fully without the camera.
The camera score alone must never trigger a high-risk alert.
6. Score fusion
The three signals are combined into one risk score. The weights below are a starting point to be tuned and described in the methodology:
final_risk = 0.5 x questionnaire + 0.3 x text sentiment + 0.2 x face emotion
Questionnaire scores use the standard PHQ-9 / GAD-7 totals, calculated with fixed formulas, not by the LLM.
The LLM is used for conversational tone and free-text sentiment only.
The face score has the lowest weight and supports a flag only when text or answers also show concern.
7. Safety and privacy
Rule-based crisis check: keyword and phrase detection on every message, independent of the LLM. If it fires, the app immediately shows a helpline (India: Tele-MANAS 14416) and alerts the counselor.
Anonymity: students are stored under an anonymous ID. The ID-to-name mapping sits in a separate collection that only the counselor role can access.
Consent: a consent screen on first use, with separate opt-in for the camera and a visible 'camera on' indicator that can be turned off at any time.
Data protection: encryption in transit and at rest; no images or video stored; only scores kept.
Disclaimer: a clear message that this is a screening aid and not a diagnosis.
